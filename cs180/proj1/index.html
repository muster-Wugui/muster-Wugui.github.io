CS 180 · Project 1

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Photo Collection

Around 1907, Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii traveled across the Russian Empire photographing scenes through three different color filters: red, green, and blue. Each scene was recorded three times on the same glass plate, with the goal of eventually reconstructing a color photograph.

1

Section 1Understanding the glass plates

In this project, I take these digitized glass plates, separate the three exposures, align them, and combine them into a single color image. I think the interesting part of the project is that the basic idea is very simple, but making it work reliably on large images requires a few important choices about alignment, image pyramids, borders, and image features.

Each scanned glass plate is one tall grayscale image containing three exposures stacked vertically. I first divide the image height into three equal sections to recover the individual color channels.

The order from top to bottom is blue, green, red, rather than RGB. This is an easy detail to get wrong. If I combine the channels in the wrong order, the resulting colors immediately look unnatural.

The three exposures were taken sequentially through different filters, so they are slightly shifted relative to one another. My goal is therefore to determine how much I need to move the green and red channels so that they align with the blue channel.

I keep the blue channel fixed and search for an x and y translation for the other two channels. Following the assumption in the project, I only consider translation and do not try to correct rotation or scaling.

2

Section 2Single-scale alignment

For the smaller JPEG images, I started with a straightforward exhaustive search.

For each channel, I try every integer displacement from −15 to +15 in both the x and y directions. This gives 961 possible shifts. For each candidate, I shift the channel using np.roll, compare it against the blue channel, and keep the displacement with the best score.

For my similarity metric, I use the L2 norm. I subtract the two images and sum the squared difference between their pixel values. A perfect match would have a score of zero, while larger differences produce larger scores.

I leave out the square root in the standard L2 formula because it does not affect which displacement has the smallest score. It only adds an unnecessary computation.

Ignoring the borders

One of the most important choices I made was excluding the borders when computing the alignment score.

The scanned plates contain strong black and white borders. If I compare the entire image, those borders can dominate the L2 score. The algorithm can then find a displacement that aligns the frame well even though the actual photograph is misaligned.

To avoid this, I ignore 20 percent of the image from each side and calculate the score only using the central region. This also prevents the wrapped pixels created by np.roll from affecting the comparison.

I think this made a much larger difference than changing the similarity metric itself.

Section 3Image pyramid alignment

The exhaustive search works well for the small JPEG images, but it becomes impractical for the full resolution TIFF files.

Each channel is around 3200 × 3600 pixels, and some of the actual displacements are close to 180 pixels. A search window of only 15 pixels is therefore not large enough.

I could simply increase the search range to around 180 pixels, but that would require evaluating roughly 130,000 candidate translations. Each candidate would also require comparing millions of pixels, so this quickly becomes too expensive.

Instead, I use an image pyramid. The basic idea is to solve the alignment problem at a small resolution first, then gradually refine the result as I return to the original resolution.

I repeatedly reduce both images by a factor of two until the shorter dimension is below 300 pixels. At this smallest level, I run the original search over a range of 15 pixels.

When I move back to the next resolution, each image dimension doubles, so I also double the displacement from the previous level. I then search only 2 pixels around that predicted location to correct any small error introduced by downsampling.

Instead of searching 961 possibilities at every resolution, the finer levels only need to test 25 possibilities.

I implemented this recursively, which I think makes the algorithm especially clean. Each call solves the smaller version of the same alignment problem before refining that estimate at the current resolution.

Why the pyramid works

Downsampling reduces the displacement at the same time that it reduces the image size.

For example, after four reductions, a channel that originally has a width around 3200 pixels becomes roughly 200 pixels wide. A displacement of 180 pixels also shrinks to around 11 pixels, which now fits comfortably inside my search range of 15 pixels.

Using the pyramid, a full resolution plate takes approximately one to two seconds to align rather than requiring an exhaustive search over the full displacement range.

Downsampling correctly

I did not reduce the image by simply selecting every second pixel.

If I discard pixels directly, high frequency details can alias into incorrect lower frequency patterns. Since the three color channels contain different intensity values, they can also alias differently. This is especially problematic because an incorrect estimate at the smallest pyramid level will be propagated upward through every later refinement.

Instead, I average each 2 × 2 block of pixels before reducing the resolution. This smooths the image before removing information and gives me a more stable coarse representation.

I compared this approach with an antialiased resize from scikit-image. My 2 × 2 averaging method differs from the reference by about 0.0127 on average, while simply selecting every second pixel differs by 0.0341. That means the direct subsampling error is about 2.7 times larger.

Choosing the pyramid parameters

I use 300 pixels as the stopping threshold for the smallest level of the pyramid.

I think there is a useful tradeoff here. The smallest image needs to be small enough that the original displacement falls inside the 15 pixel search range, but it also needs to remain large enough to preserve meaningful visual structure. If I reduce the image too far, the coarse image becomes too blurry and the first alignment estimate becomes unreliable.

For the refinement levels, I search 2 pixels around the doubled estimate. The refinement window needs to allow for small errors from downsampling and rounding, but keeping it small is important because the finest resolution contains much more data.

At full resolution, each candidate shift operates on an array of approximately 46 MB, so even a small reduction in the number of candidates makes a noticeable difference.

As a sanity check, I also ran the pyramid algorithm on the three small JPEG images. It returned exactly the same offsets as my original exhaustive search, which gave me confidence that the speedup was not changing the final alignment.

Section 4The Emir, and why pixel intensity can fail

Most of the provided images align well using the standard L2 comparison on pixel intensities. The major exception is emir.tif.

Using raw pixel intensity, the red channel was aligned to approximately (43, 61), which produced obvious colored ghosting around the portrait.

At first, I thought the issue might simply be that the three channels had very different average brightness values. However, their averages are approximately blue 0.529, green 0.489, red 0.588, so the global brightness difference is not especially large.

I think the more important problem is local image appearance.

The Emir's robe covers a large part of the image and has very different intensities between the color channels. The blue fabric appears bright through the blue filter but much darker through the red filter.

L2 compares raw intensity directly. Because of this, a large region with opposite intensity behavior can dominate the score and pull the algorithm toward a displacement that makes similar brightness values overlap rather than one that actually aligns the physical structures in the image.

Aligning with edges

To handle this case, I implemented an additional alignment method using image edges instead of raw pixel brightness.

For every pixel, I calculate its intensity difference with the neighboring pixel to the right and the neighboring pixel below it. I then combine those differences to construct an edge strength image.

This changes what the alignment algorithm sees. Instead of asking whether two locations have the same brightness, I ask whether visual boundaries occur in the same locations.

I think this makes sense for the Emir because an edge can remain in the same physical position even when the two sides of the edge have very different brightness values across color channels.

With edge based alignment, the red displacement becomes (40, 107), and the portrait becomes much sharper. The other images also remain correctly aligned, with some offsets changing by only one or two pixels.

Section 6Three images I selected

I also tested my algorithm on three additional images from the Library of Congress Prokudin-Gorskii collection.

Mozhaisk Nikolaevsky Cathedral, 1911

This image aligned very cleanly. The brickwork, tower, windows, and smaller architectural details all line up well after reconstruction.

The result is a useful example of when the translation assumption works almost exactly as expected. Source: loc.gov/item/2018679963

Vodopad Girvas, Suna River, 1915

I think this is the most interesting result among the three images I selected.

Most of the scene aligns extremely well. The rocks, trees, and wooden pole in the river are all sharp. The waterfall, however, contains obvious bands of different colors.

At first this can look like an alignment failure, but the surrounding stationary objects show that the displacement is actually correct.

The problem is that the water physically moved between the red, green, and blue exposures. Because each channel captured the water at a different moment, there is no single translation that can make the three versions of the waterfall agree.

This is a direct example of where the assumption that the channels differ only by an x and y translation stops being true. The alignment model works for the stationary parts of the scene, but it cannot undo motion that occurred between exposures. Source: loc.gov/item/2018679191

Napoleon, Waiting for Peace, 1905

This photograph of a painting also aligns very well. Fine details such as the medals, clothing, and gold decorations remain sharp.

There is also a diagonal scratch across the original glass plate. After reconstruction, the scratch appears as separate red and cyan lines rather than one aligned line.

I think this happens for a similar reason as the waterfall, although the source of the mismatch is different. The scratch belongs to the physical plate rather than the photographed scene. Because each color exposure occupies a different section of the plate, the scratch appears at a different relative position in each channel.

Aligning the actual scene therefore cannot also align the scratch. Source: loc.gov/item/2018681265

7

Wrapping upConclusion

I started this project with a simple exhaustive search over x and y translations. That approach works well for the small images, but scaling it to the full resolution scans required a coarse to fine image pyramid.

The biggest lessons for me were that the details around the basic algorithm matter a lot. Ignoring the borders prevented the score from being dominated by the plate frame, proper averaging made the pyramid more reliable, and edge based features handled cases where raw brightness was not a good representation of correspondence.

I also found the additional images interesting because they show the limits of the alignment model itself. The waterfall and the scratch cannot be fixed by finding a better translation. In both cases, the three channels contain genuinely different information, so there is no single displacement that can make every part of the image agree.
